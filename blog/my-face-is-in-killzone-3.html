My Face Is in Killzone 3

My face is in Killzone 3. I had my head scanned for that project, so I'm in the intro cinematic as a scanned character.

That was on the PS3. We've been scanning heads for almost 20 years now, and it seems like we're taking a step backwards. There's a step missing from the process right now.

My time on Killzone 3

I was an artist on Killzone 3 for about six or seven months while I worked at Sony PlayStation. I was there from around 2008 to 2010.

Most of what I did was props and some character cleanup: small props that went into the game, and touch-ups to characters and character-related assets. I think I made a bandage wound, and a few other things.

I also got to take part in cleaning up my own head scan, to learn the process a little.

How Sony did it: insourcing

Sony does a version of insourcing. They have all these support studios, or at least they did. The biggest one was VASG in San Diego. It's still there in some capacity, but it's really not doing what it used to do.

We had a giant mocap stage. Any first-party game or exclusive title got a certain level of support from Sony itself. Sony was also spearheading new technology like face scanning and cleaning up face scans, and they wanted to do that work internally, because they wanted to push the envelope.

So most games went through Sony itself: Ghost of Tsushima, Uncharted 2, 3 and 4, Death Stranding 1. If work was farmed out to another place like Virtuos, that was really just to support the insourcing.

The process had to be invented in-house

When I was there, they wanted a certain level of control over everything, and they wanted everything to look a very certain way. We weren't just making games. We were showcasing technology and being at the cutting edge.

Back then, you couldn't just scan something and send it off for somebody else to clean up. You had to invent that process, figure out what worked, and teach it to the internal artists before you could ever give it to an external studio or send it to China or India. All of it had to be set up in-house and done in-house to hit a certain quality bar.

All the heads in the last SOCOM game that released were head scans too. And this was all happening on the PS3.

Why head scans look like a step backwards

There's really no difference between how heads were being made then and how they're being made now. The biggest thing I think people notice is that we've been doing this for almost 20 years, and it seems like we're going backwards.

I'd say that has less to do with any sort of malice, and more to do with how the industry has been changing and what the goals are for developers now.

What scanning was for

Before, you scanned people to capture a lot of detail you didn't want to create from scratch, especially the diffuse: pores and all of those things. Painting that stuff was a huge time sink, even more than sculpting.

And with sculpting, you're trying to capture a certain likeness from your references. Scanning just got you there much faster.

Characterization is the missing step

But then you had to take the scan and turn it into a character. That process is called characterization. Some people think of it as idealization.

Really, it's shaping the character to fit the narrative, and pushing their features a little beyond where they are into something a little more fantastic.

The same principle applies in painting and drawing. You're supposed to emphasize and embellish what's there, not change it. And you definitely don't want things to look too soft or too undefined.

You see a lot more softness and undefined areas in real life, but in 3D you can't settle for that. You have to push it one step further, so it gets out of this weird valley of softness and into something that is more character.

Nobody's art directing it anymore

This is what's missing from the process right now. The scans are so good that studios are just accepting the scan and moving on, and nobody's really art directing the process anymore.

A lot of those people have left and moved to other studios. For the ones who are still there, the marching orders have changed. There isn't much emphasis on idealization or characterization. It's about getting these assets done to a minimum viable quality and moving on, because there are just so many of them to do.

The day Sony gave us all a copy

Killzone 3 was one of the most interesting games I worked on, because we got to work with Guerrilla's tech, and it was such a big studio release.

There was a point where they weren't sure if they were going to give everybody at the studio a copy of the game. Everybody was so passionate about it and had worked so hard on it. People voiced their discontent, and sure enough, Sony gave us all copies of the game we'd poured our heart and soul into.
