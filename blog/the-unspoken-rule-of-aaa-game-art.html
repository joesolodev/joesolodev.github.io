The Unspoken Rule of AAA Game Art

Here's something nobody in AAA will say out loud: the better you are at painting and drawing as a 3D artist, the less the other 3D artists like you.

Today is day 83 of my move from AAA game development to indie. It's been an exciting stretch, and it's made a few things clear to me that I couldn't see from inside a big studio. That rule is one of them.

Indie is what I always pictured

Believe it or not, I always wanted to be an indie developer. I spent my whole career in AAA, and it never quite matched what I thought it would be. Not once, really.

Indie is different. The indie space is almost exactly what I imagined, which honestly surprised me. I expected it to be a lot harder.

What I mean by "what I imagined" comes down to two things: how much you get to collaborate with people, and how many hats you get to wear.

Fundamentals still matter

I trained to paint and draw at an atelier, and I still believe you need to be a fundamentally good artist. Not an okay artist. A good one.

That means you can draw decently and you can paint a picture. I'm not talking about masterpieces. I'm talking about whether you can paint an orange with lighting: a nice one-point light and a cast shadow. Can you do decent gestures? Can you do a basic two-value or three-value lay-in, with a little middle shading in there?

That's where I've spent my time. And because I spent my time there, I picked up a lot of skills beyond making characters.

The skills AAA never let me use

Most of those extra skills I never got to use in a AAA environment. I was a writer all through high school. I did journalism. I honestly thought I was going to be a writer for a living.

But nobody at a AAA studio is going to let you near the story unless that's your job title. And it's not just story. I can do all of these things, and in a AAA studio I'm not allowed to do any of them.

The unspoken rule

In fact, being a good traditional artist is kind of frowned upon. Some people will tell you that's not true. It is.

The more you know about painting and drawing as a 3D artist, the more other 3D artists dislike you. There's a secret, unspoken thing happening in this industry: a lot of people just don't want to learn their fundamentals, and they don't really jibe with the people who did.

I don't know where it came from. It's probably an ego thing. But it's there, and if you've worked in a big studio, you've probably felt it.

Using everything I know

I don't have to deal with any of that now. Not on the indie team I'm working with, and not on my own team for my own game.

Right now I'm working for two teams: one for somebody else and one for myself. On both, I get to use every skill I've learned, all the time. It's amazing.

If you're not being used, go build something

You might be in the same spot: you have the skills, and nobody's using them. If that's you, find a group that actually wants your skills, or start your own indie project.

This is where a little bit of AI comes in. Now you can put your money where your mouth is. Need some code? Find open source code if you want to do it more ethically, and start programming your own tools. Start programming your own game. Start programming all the stuff people have stopped you from doing.

If programming a game is the difference between getting a story you wrote out into the world or not, do it. It's fun. It's awesome. I'm having a great time.

More from the rest of the journey soon. Check back here for regular blog updates.

Work hard, don't suck.
